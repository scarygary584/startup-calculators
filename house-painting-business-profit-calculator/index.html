Plan labor costs, material costs, and overhead

A painting company needs more than a selling price to judge whether a project is worthwhile. Start with one typical job, enter the resources it will use, and then test how many similar jobs fit into a month. This painting business profit calculator separates direct expenses from monthly overhead so you can see where the money goes.

The starting figures are editable examples, not local market prices or a forecast of what painting businesses earn. Replace them with supplier quotes, your own time records, and a realistic schedule. Use the same measurement basis for the area and the price: a rate based on painted wall area should not be multiplied by floor area.

Build a complete job cost

  • Materials: Include coatings, primer, caulk, masking tape, roller covers, abrasives, and other consumables. Add purchases once; avoid counting the same supplies again under travel and prep.
  • Labor: Enter total person-hours for everyone on the crew. Two painters working eight hours each means 16 hours. Include preparation, application, setup, cleanup, and expected touch-ups.
  • Hourly rate: Use your actual labor expense per person-hour. If crew members have different rates, use a weighted average. Include applicable employment-related costs in your estimate.
  • Travel and prep supplies: Account for trips, deliveries, and job-specific extras that have not been included elsewhere. A lift rental or unusual access expense also needs a place in the budget.
  • Monthly overhead: Include recurring expenses such as insurance, software, advertising, storage, and vehicle costs. Keep these separate from costs already assigned to individual jobs.

If you do the work yourself, assign a value to your time in the labor field. Otherwise, the result combines compensation for your work with the return from owning the business. You can compare both approaches, but keep the meaning of the result clear.

Check capacity before raising the monthly total

More bookings only help if you can complete them. Multiply person-hours per job by jobs per month, then compare that workload with available crew time. Leave room for estimates, purchasing, travel, scheduling, and unpaid administrative work. Contractors handling both residential and commercial work may want to run each type separately because crew requirements and payment timing can differ.

Revenue, project cost, and the profit formula

The calculator uses the following steps. The job result is the amount left after the direct expenses you enter. Monthly overhead is deducted afterward, which is why the displayed job margin is not the same as the final business margin.

  1. Job revenue = painted square feet × price per square foot.
  2. Direct project cost = materials + (labor hours × hourly labor rate) + travel and prep supplies.
  3. Job profit before overhead = job revenue − direct project cost.
  4. Monthly profit = job profit × completed jobs per month − monthly overhead.
  5. Job profit margin = job profit ÷ job revenue × 100.
  6. Simple equipment payback = startup/equipment cost ÷ positive monthly profit.

A worked example using the starting inputs

Suppose a paint job covers 1,800 square feet at $2.75 per square foot. The resulting invoice amount is $4,950. Materials are $950, 38 person-hours at $28 cost $1,064, and travel and prep supplies add $150. Those direct expenses total $2,164, leaving $2,786 before monthly overhead.

Four identical jobs produce $11,144 before overhead. Subtracting the example $1,200 monthly overhead leaves $9,944. The displayed job margin is 56.3%, while the monthly margin after that overhead is about 50.2%. Neither figure is an industry benchmark, and taxes or expenses omitted from the inputs are not deducted.

At those assumptions, $6,500 of equipment has a simple payback of about 0.7 months. This is arithmetic, not a promise of fast recovery: it assumes the modeled work is completed, payment is collected, and the estimated monthly result is available to recover that investment. Try a slower month before relying on the payback figure.

Test a slower schedule and a longer job

Change four jobs to two and the monthly result becomes $4,372 with the same overhead. Alternatively, keeping four jobs but adding ten person-hours to each reduces the monthly result by $1,120 at the example hourly rate. Small differences in preparation time can matter across a full schedule. Save your own low, expected, and busy-month inputs so you can compare plans consistently.

Set a target margin for a painting estimate

Markup and margin describe different calculations. Markup is the amount added to cost, expressed as a percentage of that cost. Margin is the amount left from the selling price, expressed as a percentage of the selling price. A 25% markup does not produce a 25% margin.

For example, a $2,000 total cost with 25% markup gives a $2,500 price and a 20% margin. To achieve a 25% margin on that same cost, divide $2,000 by 0.75, giving approximately $2,666.67. The general formula is selling price = total cost ÷ (1 − target margin as a decimal). Use a target below 100%.

Include a reasonable overhead allocation in total cost when setting a business-level margin target. With the starting inputs, allocating $1,200 across four jobs adds $300 to each, making the modeled cost $2,464 per job. Dividing that by 0.75 gives about $3,285.33 for an illustrative 25% margin after the allocated overhead. This is a planning example, not a recommended quote.

You can use the tool as a margin calculator by adjusting the selling rate and watching the result. Remember that its displayed percentage is before monthly overhead. Your actual estimate also depends on the agreed scope, access, surface condition, finish requirements, and customer expectations. A measured area alone cannot describe every project.

Review painting business ROI over a defined period

Simple payback tells you how long modeled earnings would take to match the equipment outlay. Return on investment asks how much net gain you have earned relative to the investment over a stated period. For a basic planning comparison, divide the net gain attributable to the investment by its cost and multiply by 100. Define which expenses you deducted and avoid treating sales as net gain.

Do not automatically multiply a strong month by twelve. Build a month-by-month estimate if the schedule changes through the year. Equipment replacement, financing, taxes, and money reserved for operations can change how much cash is available to the owner.

Painting business calculator questions

Should prep time be included?

Yes. Washing, sanding, caulking, masking, moving furnishings, setup, and cleanup all use paid time. Add anticipated touch-ups as well. Track estimated and actual hours after each completed job so the next estimate reflects your own working pace.

Does this work for interior and exterior painting?

Yes, provided you enter the appropriate area, selling rate, and expenses. Exterior work may involve access equipment, weather interruptions, or more surface preparation. Run separate scenarios when the requirements differ rather than assuming the same cost per square foot applies everywhere.

Does the paint estimator update the main calculator automatically?

No. Review its gallons and supplies estimate, then click “Use this total in profit calculator” to copy the total into the materials field. That replaces the existing materials amount. Add any other project materials that the estimate has not covered, and check the product's stated coverage.

Is monthly profit the amount I can take home?

Not necessarily. It is the remainder after the expenses entered in this model. It does not separately calculate income taxes, financing, or cash reserves. Owner labor is only included if you allow for it in the labor inputs. Unpaid invoices can also make available cash lower than the modeled result.

How many jobs cover the monthly overhead cost?

Divide monthly overhead by the positive contribution from one job, then round up to a whole job. Using the starting inputs, $1,200 divided by $2,786 is about 0.43, so one completed and paid example job covers that overhead in the model. If the contribution is zero or negative, adding more identical jobs will not cover overhead.

What should I compare before accepting a project?

Check the scope, total person-hours, materials, travel, and available calendar time. Compare the expected return with other work you could do in that period. After completion, record actual spending and hours alongside the estimate. That gives your next decision a stronger basis than a generic earnings claim.
